Before AI Can Transform,
the Ground Must Be Ready
AI does not create order out of chaos — it amplifies whatever already exists. Organizations that deploy AI on top of poorly defined processes, unclassified data, and weak governance will get faster, larger versions of their existing problems. These seven prerequisite domains are not optional steps you can skip. They are the foundation that makes AI investment safe, sustainable, and transformational rather than expensive and risky.
AI Amplifies What Exists
AI models learn from your data and automate your processes. If those are broken, biased, or undocumented, AI scales the problem — not the solution. Clean foundations produce clean outcomes.
Governance Failures Are Costly
Regulatory fines, data breaches, and reputational damage from AI missteps are far more expensive than building the right guardrails before launch. Governance is not a brake — it's a seatbelt.
Speed Comes From Readiness
Teams that invest in prerequisites move faster once AI deployment begins — fewer blockers, fewer rollbacks, faster adoption. Skipping this step creates the slowdowns it was trying to avoid.

The 7 Foundation Domains
7 domains
Domain 01
Process Maturity
Defined, documented & measurable workflows
Why AI needs this
AI automates, augments, or optimizes processes. If a process is not documented — if people perform it differently each time, or it only exists in the heads of experienced employees — there is nothing concrete for AI to learn from or act on. Undefined processes produce AI that is either inconsistent, impossible to validate, or actively harmful to quality.
What to have in place
Key processes documented — step-by-step, with decision points, inputs, and outputs clearly defined.

Process owners assigned — each process has a named owner responsible for its quality and accuracy.

Performance baselines established — current cycle time, error rate, or cost is known, so AI improvement can be measured.

Exception paths mapped — edge cases and exception-handling logic are documented, not just the happy path.
Key artifacts
Process maps / SOPs RACI charts Process KPIs & baselines Exception & escalation guides Process owner register
Common gap signals
Tribal knowledge is the primary process documentation · Different teams do the same task differently · No one can describe where a process starts and ends · Outcomes are measured, but the process producing them is not.
Domain 02
Data Foundation & Classification
Inventoried, classified & quality-assured data
Why AI needs this
Data is the fuel for every AI system. Models trained on incomplete, inaccurate, or unclassified data produce unreliable outputs. Without data classification, you cannot know which data an AI is allowed to access, which must stay private, and which requires special handling. Without a data inventory, AI projects will constantly stall on "where does this data live?" and "is this data trustworthy?"
What to have in place
Data inventory — a catalogue of all major datasets: location, owner, format, update frequency, and quality status.

Data classification scheme — at minimum: Public · Internal · Confidential · Restricted/PII. Each dataset classified and labeled.

Data quality standards — completeness, accuracy, consistency, and timeliness targets defined per dataset.

Data lineage — for critical datasets, the origin, transformations, and consumption points are documented.

Data ownership — every dataset has a named owner accountable for its quality and classification.
Key artifacts
Data catalogue / inventory Classification policy Data quality scorecard Data lineage diagrams Data ownership register Labeling standards
Common gap signals
No one knows what data exists or where · Sensitive data is in spreadsheets with no access controls · Different systems hold conflicting versions of the same record · Data quality varies wildly across teams.
Domain 03
Information Security
Policies, controls & access governance
Why AI needs this
AI systems often have broad access to data across an organization — far broader than any individual user. Without strong information security, an AI model can become a data exfiltration vector, an attack surface for adversarial inputs, or a compliance liability. Security must be designed into AI systems from day one, not bolted on after an incident.
What to have in place
Information security policy — formally documented and approved, covering data handling, access, and incident response.

Identity & Access Management (IAM) — role-based access control (RBAC) enforced; least-privilege principle applied to all AI service accounts and integrations.

Secure development practices — AI model training pipelines, APIs, and deployment environments follow secure coding and configuration standards.

Secrets management — API keys, credentials, and tokens used by AI systems are stored in vaults, never hardcoded.

Incident response plan — a documented, tested plan for AI-related security events including model poisoning and data leakage.
Key artifacts
Information Security Policy IAM / RBAC matrix Least-privilege standards Secrets management solution Incident response plan Pen-test / security review process
Common gap signals
Shared logins used by AI services · API keys in source code repositories · No formal review process before a new AI tool is connected to internal data · No one is responsible for AI security posture.
Domain 04
Privacy & Regulatory Compliance
PII mapping, legal landscape & review capability
Why AI needs this
AI systems that process personal data are subject to privacy regulations (GDPR, CCPA, HIPAA, and equivalents). AI that makes decisions affecting individuals — hiring, lending, insurance, healthcare — faces specific legal scrutiny around algorithmic accountability. Without a compliance baseline, AI deployment can create legal liability overnight, particularly when models are exposed externally.
What to have in place
PII mapping — all personal data the organization holds is identified, mapped to its purpose, and has a legal basis for processing.

Regulatory landscape documented — the applicable regulations (by region, industry, and data type) are known and monitored for changes.

Privacy by design standard — data minimization and purpose limitation are applied by default when building AI systems.

Legal review capability — a process exists to route new AI use cases through legal and compliance review before deployment.

Data retention & deletion policies — including how to honor right-to-erasure requests when data has been used to train a model.
Key artifacts
Data Processing Register (ROPA) PII inventory & map Privacy policy & notices Regulatory compliance matrix AI legal review checklist Consent management framework
Common gap signals
No one knows which regulations apply to a new AI use case · Customer PII flows freely through internal tools without consent tracking · Legal is not consulted until after an AI system is built · No process for handling AI data deletion requests.
Domain 05
Technology & Integration Baseline
Cloud readiness, APIs & platform standards
Why AI needs this
AI systems do not run in isolation — they need to read from and write to existing systems, consume data from multiple sources, and operate reliably at scale. Without a clear technology baseline, every AI project starts by re-solving the same integration problems. Inconsistent cloud platforms, undocumented APIs, and absent identity standards add months to every AI deployment and create fragile, hard-to-maintain systems.
What to have in place
Cloud platform decision — the organization has selected and standardized on a cloud provider (or hybrid strategy), with AI/ML services available and approved.

API standards & catalogue — internal APIs are documented, versioned, and follow a consistent standard (REST/GraphQL/gRPC). A service catalogue exists.

Identity platform — a single identity and SSO layer that AI systems can integrate with for authentication and authorization.

Observability standards — logging, monitoring, and alerting standards that AI services must follow to be production-ready.

Deployment pipeline — CI/CD practices that include security scanning, testing gates, and approval workflows before promoting AI models to production.
Key artifacts
Cloud architecture decision API catalogue & standards Identity / SSO platform CI/CD pipeline standards Observability framework Infrastructure-as-code templates
Common gap signals
Every AI project requires a custom integration from scratch · Internal APIs are undocumented or inconsistent · No logging or monitoring standards mean AI systems run blind · Cloud usage is uncoordinated across teams.
Domain 06
People & Change Readiness
AI literacy, buy-in & change capability
Why AI needs this
The most technically perfect AI system fails if people won't use it, don't understand it, or actively resist it. AI transformation is fundamentally a people change programme. Without baseline AI literacy, employees cannot make good decisions about when to trust AI output, when to override it, and how to identify errors. Without leadership buy-in and change management capability, adoption stalls at the pilot stage.
What to have in place
AI literacy baseline — all employees have a foundational understanding of what AI is, what it can and cannot do, and how it will affect their roles.

Leadership sponsorship — named executives own and visibly champion the AI transformation with resources, decisions, and communications.

Change management capability — an established approach (internal team or partner) for managing organizational change, communication, and adoption.

Reskilling pathways — a plan for upskilling roles that will be most affected by AI, so transformation feels like growth, not threat.

Feedback mechanisms — channels for employees to raise concerns, report issues with AI systems, and contribute improvement ideas.
Key artifacts
AI literacy curriculum Executive sponsorship charter Change management plan Stakeholder impact assessment Reskilling roadmap Employee feedback channels
Common gap signals
Employees view AI as a threat to their jobs with no counter-narrative · AI initiatives are IT-led with no business ownership · No training plan exists beyond vendor demos · Leadership endorses AI in town halls but doesn't fund it in planning.
Domain 07
Ethics & AI Principles
Values, accountability & responsible use standards
Why AI needs this
AI systems make decisions and take actions at a scale and speed no human team can match. Without explicit ethical principles and accountability structures, AI will optimize for the wrong objectives, embed and amplify bias, and make decisions that are impossible to explain or contest. Ethics is not a values exercise — it is a risk management requirement that protects both the people affected by AI and the organization deploying it.
What to have in place
Adopted AI principles — the organization has formally articulated its AI values: fairness, transparency, accountability, human oversight, and explainability at minimum.

AI ethics owner — a named role or committee responsible for reviewing AI decisions against the organization's principles.

Bias assessment process — a repeatable methodology for testing models for demographic bias, proxy discrimination, and distributional shift before deployment.

Explainability standards — for AI decisions affecting individuals, the organization can provide a meaningful explanation of how the outcome was reached.

Escalation & override paths — clear, accessible ways for affected individuals and internal teams to flag, challenge, or override AI outputs.
Key artifacts
AI Ethics Policy AI Principles charter Bias testing methodology Explainability standard Ethics review board / TOR Override & appeal process
Common gap signals
No one can articulate what "responsible AI" means in practice for this organization · Models are deployed without bias testing · Affected individuals have no way to contest an AI decision · Ethics discussions happen only after a problem surfaces publicly.
